There is a particular failure mode that happens after a week or so of drinking
nothing but very light roasts. Everything starts tasting like the same acidic
tea. Fruit notes that were vivid on day one collapse into a general brightness,
and you find yourself ordering a third filter to see whether the problem is the
coffee or you.

It is you. Or rather, it is calibration.

## The problem with drinking only one thing

Nordic-style light roasting is a genuine achievement, and the bars that do it
best — Tim Wendelboe in Oslo, The Barn in Berlin — are not being precious for
its own sake. Roast pale enough and a good lot will taste of the fruit it
actually is instead of the caramelisation you applied to it. That is a real
gain, and it is why those rooms have been so influential.

But a palate needs contrast to stay sharp. Drink only in that register and the
register stops registering. The tasting notes on the board become a reading
comprehension exercise rather than a description.

## What fixed it

A standing bar in Rome, near the Pantheon, two euros, drunk in ninety seconds
while leaning on a marble counter.

Sant'Eustachio's house coffee is dark, whipped with sugar before it reaches
you, and by every metric the specialty world uses it is doing the wrong thing.
It is also delicious, and more importantly it is *different* — enough contrast
to reset the baseline. The next light filter I drank, two days later, tasted
like something again.

## The practical version

If you are on a long coffee trip, deliberately break the pattern:

- Drink one dark, traditional espresso every few days. Not as a treat, as a
  reference point.
- Order the same coffee as both espresso and filter when a bar offers it.
  Prufrock in London is set up for exactly this.
- Take a day off entirely. It works better than any of the above and nobody
  wants to hear it.

The goal is not to decide which tradition is correct. It is to keep being able
to tell them apart.
